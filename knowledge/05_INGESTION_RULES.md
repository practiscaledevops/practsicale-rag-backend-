I wanted to do big things.

But there were moments when everything seemed to be going wrong at the
same time.

Accounts got closed. Money got stuck. Disputes happened. The company
needed me. People needed answers.

And one day, I even broke down in front of a client.

I'm not ashamed of that.

We're human.

You can have a huge vision and still have days when you don't know what
to do next.

You can lead hundreds of people and still need to tell someone:

"I'm going through something right now. Please don't push me any
further."

The dangerous part isn't crying.

The dangerous part is making permanent decisions about your dreams while
you're living through temporary chaos.

Some people will see you struggling and tell you:

"Maybe you're trying to do too much."

"Maybe you should think smaller."

No.

Maybe I don't need a smaller dream.

Maybe I just need to survive this chapter.

Don't measure the size of your future by the difficulty of your current
day.

Comfort zone doesn't always look like laziness.

Sometimes it looks like:

"We have 40 clients." "Our team is full." "Recruitment isn't a problem
anymore." "This department is running fine."

Good.

Now create the next problem.

Fill one office? Open another. Build one team? Build the next. Master
one role? Prepare for the next. Hit one target? Raise it.

Growth happens when you deliberately step outside what you've already
mastered.

And sometimes the people who push you into that uncomfortable zone are
the people you'll respect the most later.

Don't wait for growth to make you uncomfortable. Choose discomfort
before you're forced into it.

I don't want my team working 12-hour days.

If someone consistently needs 12 hours to finish a 9-hour role, I don't
automatically see commitment. I see a system I need to investigate.

Maybe we're understaffed. Maybe one person is doing four people's jobs.
Maybe they haven't learned how to delegate. Maybe knowledge is trapped
inside one person.

I had a manager doing the work of multiple people. When she left, we
built a team of six to properly handle the workload.

That taught me something:

Don't build heroes. Build teams.

Your best people shouldn't have to carry the company on their backs.

Teach. Delegate. Hire. Build the next person.

That's how companies scale without burning out the people who helped
build them.

There are only two directions in a growing company:

You Go or You Grow.

Managing 100 people successfully? Good.

Can you manage 200?

Mastered 200? Can you build the systems and leaders required for 500?

Once your current role becomes comfortable, start preparing for the next
one.

Your title doesn't need to change every month. Your capacity should.

Learn more. Lead more. Build more leaders. Handle bigger problems.

Because if the company keeps growing, its people have to grow with it.

You Go or You Grow.
